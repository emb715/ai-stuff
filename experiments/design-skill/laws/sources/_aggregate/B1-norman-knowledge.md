# B1 Norman — Patterns, Heuristics, Antipatterns

Extracted from *The Design of Everyday Things* (Donald Norman, revised edition). All quotes are verbatim from the source extract. Laws already captured (D-1 affordances, D-2 feedback, D-19 gulf of execution, D-21 error inevitability, D-27 conceptual model, D-28 discoverability, D-34 natural mapping) are not re-extracted here.

## Patterns

### Vertical Plate Signifier for Push Doors
- **Description:** Use a flat vertical plate on the side of a door to be pushed; the plate's perceived affordance unambiguously signals "push" without labels. Pair with visible pillars/hinges where helpful.
- **Source quote:** "With doors that push, the designer must provide signals that naturally indicate where to push. These need not destroy the aesthetics. Put a vertical plate on the side to be pushed. Or make the supporting pillars visible. The vertical plate and supporting pillars are natural signals, naturally interpreted, making it easy to know just what to do: no labels needed." (Chapter 1: The Psychopathology of Everyday Things)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-28, D-34

### Panic Bar (Forcing Function for Egress)
- **Description:** A wide horizontal bar across an exit door doubles as an unambiguous push signifier and a physical constraint that lets panicked crowds escape by leaning on it; legally required in many public buildings.
- **Source quote:** "in many countries, the law requires doors in public places to open outward, and moreover to be operated by so-called panic bars, so that they automatically open when people, in a panic to escape a fire, push their bodies against them. This is a great application of appropriate affordances" (Chapter 2: The Psychology of Everyday Actions)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-21

### Seat-Control Shape Mapping
- **Description:** Make a control resemble the thing it controls (e.g., car seat switch shaped like a seat), so operation is read directly from spatial analogy with no labels or instruction.
- **Source quote:** "This is an excellent example of natural mapping. The control is in the shape of the seat itself: the mapping is straightforward. To move the front edge of the seat higher, lift up on the front part of the button. To make the seat back recline, move the button back." (Chapter 1, Figure 1.7 caption)
- **Relates to:** D-34

### Spatial Switch-to-Light Mapping
- **Description:** Arrange controls in the same 2-D layout as the devices they control (lights in a room, windows in a car) so the mapping is carried by the world and no memorization is required.
- **Source quote:** "Why not place the switches horizontally, in exact analogy to the things being controlled, with a two-dimensional layout so that the switches can be placed on a floor plan of the building in exact correspondence to the areas that they control? Match the layout of the lights with the layout of the switches: the principle of natural mapping." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-28, D-34

### Forcing Function (Sequence Interlock)
- **Description:** Block the next stage of an action until a prior condition is satisfied, so an omitted prerequisite becomes physically impossible (e.g., car won't leave Park without brake applied; microwave door kills power when opened).
- **Source quote:** "An interlock forces operations to take place in proper sequence. Microwave ovens and devices with interior exposure to high voltage use interlocks as forcing functions to prevent people from opening the door of the oven or disassembling the devices without first turning off the electric power" (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Lock-In (Prevent Premature Exit)
- **Description:** Keep an operation active and prompt on attempted exit, so the user cannot accidentally destroy unsaved work; make the confirm dialog also a convenient save shortcut.
- **Source quote:** "A lock-in keeps an operation active, preventing someone from prematurely stopping it. Standard lock-ins exist on many computer applications, where any attempt to exit the application without saving work is prevented by a message prompt asking whether that is what is really wanted" (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-21

### Lockout (Prevent Entry/Event)
- **Description:** Physically block access to a hazardous state or space until a precondition is met (stairwell gate past ground floor, fire-extinguisher pin, childproof cap) to prevent accidental triggering.
- **Source quote:** "A lockout prevents someone from entering a space that is dangerous, or prevents an event from occurring... The pin that prevents a fire extinguisher from being activated until it is removed is a lockout forcing function to prevent accidental discharge." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Tilted Anti-Affordance Surface
- **Description:** Slope or otherwise deform a surface so it cannot be used as a shelf, preventing unwanted behavior (placing cups on controls) without a sign.
- **Source quote:** "The plate was tilted rather than horizontal to discourage people (us or visitors) from placing objects, such as cups, on the plate: an example of an anti-affordance." (Chapter 4, Figure 4.5 caption)
- **Relates to:** D-1

### Accidental Signifiers (Trails and Discards)
- **Description:** Use incidental traces left in the world (footprints in snow, a discarded cup on a ledge) as deliberate-or-not signifiers that guide others' behavior; design can anticipate and amplify these.
- **Source quote:** "flat surfaces afford support, and as soon as one person discovers it can be used to dispose of empty drink containers, the discarded container becomes a signifier, telling others that it is permissible to discard their items there." (Chapter 1, Figure 1.5 caption)
- **Relates to:** D-28

### Rubber-Pipe False Anti-Affordance
- **Description:** Use a visually strong anti-affordance (blocking-looking pipes) made of a yielding material so authorized users can pass while casual users are deterred — a permission control via signifier.
- **Source quote:** "the pipes were made of rubber, so vehicles could simply drive right over them. A very clever signifier, signaling a blocked road (via an apparent anti-affordance) to the average person, but permitting passage for those who knew." (Chapter 1)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-28

### Permissive Input Parsing
- **Description:** Accept any intelligible format for dates, times, phone numbers, etc. and transform internally, so the user never has to learn an arbitrary machine format.
- **Source quote:** "Why not figure out the variety of ways a person might fill out a form and accommodate all of them?... it is possible to specify dates any way you like: 'November 23, 2015,' '23 Nov. 15,' or '11.23.15.' It even accepts phrases such as 'a week from Thursday'" (Chapter 2)
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Explicit Conceptual Model Display (Nest Thermostat)
- **Description:** Show current state, target state, direction of change, and ETA on a small display so the user forms an accurate mental model of what the system is doing and why.
- **Source quote:** "when it has to change the room temperature substantially... it gives a prediction: 'Now 75°, will be 72° in 20 minutes.'... aiding the home occupant's development of a conceptual model both of Nest and also of the home's energy consumption." (Chapter 2)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-27

### Two-Component Reminders (Signal + Message)
- **Description:** A complete reminder must contain both a signal (something is to be remembered) and the message (what to remember); design both, since notes alone or pings alone fail predictably.
- **Source quote:** "The ideal reminder has to have both components: the signal that something is to be remembered, and then the message of what it is." (Chapter 3)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-28

### Forcing Function to Prevent Left-Behind Items
- **Description:** Sequence operations so the user cannot reach the goal without retrieving the prone-to-forgetting item (ATM card must be removed before money is dispensed).
- **Source quote:** "automated teller machines often require removal of the bank card before delivering the requested money: this prevents forgetting the bank card, capitalizing on the fact that people seldom forget the goal of the activity" (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Poka-Yoke (Error-Proofing Constraints)
- **Description:** Add small fixtures, asymmetrical holes, colored parts, or covers so that misassembly or accidental activation is physically blocked — combine constraints + forcing functions + signifiers.
- **Source quote:** "Poka-yoke translates as 'error proofing' or 'avoiding error.' One of the techniques of poka-yoke is to add simple fixtures, jigs, or devices to constrain the operations so that they are correct." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-21

### Undo as Primary Error Recovery
- **Description:** Provide multi-level undo so every reversible action can be taken back; treat it as the default recovery path, not a nice-to-have.
- **Source quote:** "Perhaps the most powerful tool to minimize the impact of errors is the Undo command in modern electronic systems, reversing the operations performed by the previous command, wherever possible. The best systems have multiple levels of undoing" (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-21

### Sensibility Checks Before Destructive Actions
- **Description:** Have the system flag values wildly out of expected range (1000× drug dose, million-dollar transfer entered in wrong currency field) before executing, instead of meekly complying.
- **Source quote:** "Even simple checks for reasonableness would have stopped all of these errors... Electronic systems have another advantage over mechanical ones: they can check to make sure that the requested operation is sensible." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Multi-Modal Feedback to Avoid Interference
- **Description:** Distribute feedback across modalities (visual + auditory + haptic) so that high-load primary modality is not degraded and warnings still register.
- **Source quote:** "To maximize efficiency of working memory it is best to present different information over different modalities: sight, sound, touch (haptics), hearing, spatial location, and gestures. Automobiles should use auditory presentation of driving instructions and haptic vibration..." (Chapter 3)
- **Relates to:** D-2

### Naturalistic Sound Signifiers
- **Description:** Use sounds that reflect the physical source and its materials (click of a bolt, whistle of a kettle, rattle of loose parts) instead of generic beeps, so the sound carries information about what is happening.
- **Source quote:** "Sounds should be generated so as to give knowledge about the source. They should convey something about the actions that are taking place, actions that matter to the user but that would otherwise not be visible." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-28

### Skeuomorphic Bridge for New Tech
- **Description:** Borrow the form of an old, familiar artifact (file folders, horseless carriage) when introducing a new technology so existing conceptual models transfer; discard skeuomorphs once users are fluent.
- **Source quote:** "Skeuomorphic is the technical term for incorporating old, familiar ideas into new technologies, even though they no longer play a functional role... One way of overcoming the fear of the new is to make it look like the old. This practice... gives comfort and makes learning easier. Existing conceptual models need only be modified rather than replaced." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-27

### Activity-Centered Controls
- **Description:** Group controls by the activity the user is performing (lecture, video,投影), not by the device (lights, sound, projector), so the user doesn't context-switch between screens mid-task.
- **Source quote:** "Activity-centered controls are the proper way to go, if the activities are carefully selected to match actual requirements. But even in these cases, manual controls will still be required because there will always be some new, unexpected demand that requires idiosyncratic settings." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-27, D-28

### Universal / Inclusive Design (Side-Benefit Pattern)
- **Description:** Design for the extremes (arthritis, low vision) and the result benefits everyone; large high-contrast type, adjustable heights, and flexible options all illustrate "design for the edges, help the middle."
- **Source quote:** "Designing for people with special needs is often called inclusive or universal design. Those names are fitting, for it is often the case that everyone benefits. Make the lettering larger, with high-contrast type, and everyone can read it better." (Chapter 6)
- **Relates to:** D-28

### Deliberate Difficulty (Reverse-Usability Pattern)
- **Description:** When access must be restricted (school doors for handicapped children, safes, dangerous equipment), systematically invert usability rules: hide components, use unnatural mappings, require simultaneous distant actions, give no feedback.
- **Source quote:** "This was good design. The door was at a school for handicapped children, and the school didn't want the children to be able to get out to the street without an adult. Only adults were large enough to operate the two latches. Violating the rules of ease of use is just what was needed." (Chapter 6)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-28

### Standardization as Last Resort
- **Description:** When natural mapping and in-world knowledge can't solve a consistent interaction problem across vendors, standardize the convention so people learn once and transfer; standardize the psychological model, not the mechanics.
- **Source quote:** "If all else fails, standardize... Standardization is indeed the fundamental principle of desperation: when no other solution appears possible, simply design everything the same way, so people only have to learn once." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-27, D-28

## Heuristics

### Seven-Stage Question Check
- **Check:** For each of the seven stages — Goal, Plan, Specify, Perform, Perceive, Interpret, Compare — can the user answer the corresponding question with the information the system provides?
- **Source quote:** "Anyone using a product should always be able to determine the answers to all seven questions. This puts the burden on the designer to ensure that at each stage, the product provides the information required to answer the question." (Chapter 2)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-19, D-27, D-28

### Discoverability Check
- **Check:** Can a first-time user determine what actions are possible, where and how to perform them, and what state the device is in — without a manual?
- **Source quote:** "Discoverability: Is it possible to even figure out what actions are possible and where and how to perform them? Understanding: What does it all mean? How is the product supposed to be used?" (Chapter 1)
- **Relates to:** D-28, D-1, D-2

### Feedback Quality Check
- **Check:** Is feedback immediate, informative, prioritized, and unobtrusive — does each action confirm receipt, convey what happened, and avoid drowning out critical signals?
- **Source quote:** "Feedback must be immediate... Feedback must also be informative... Feedback must also be prioritized, so that unimportant information is presented in an unobtrusive fashion, but important signals are presented in a way that does capture attention." (Chapter 1)
- **Relates to:** D-2

### Conceptual Model Coherence Check
- **Check:** Does the system image (controls + labels + docs + behavior) suggest a single, consistent conceptual model that matches the actual mechanism — or does it mislead?
- **Source quote:** "When the system image is incoherent or inappropriate, as in the case of the refrigerator, then the user cannot easily use the device. If it is incomplete or contradictory, there will be trouble." (Chapter 1)
- **Relates to:** D-27

### Mapping Naturalness Check
- **Check:** Does the relationship between controls and outcomes exploit spatial analogy (best: mounted on the item; second: adjacent; third: same spatial configuration)? If not, are labels really necessary?
- **Source quote:** "Here are three levels of mapping, arranged in decreasing effectiveness as memory aids: Best mapping: Controls are mounted directly on the item to be controlled. Second-best: Controls are as close as possible... Third-best: Controls are arranged in the same spatial configuration as the objects to be controlled." (Chapter 3)
- **Relates to:** D-34

### Signifier-vs-Affordance Audit
- **Check:** Are the signifiers (where to act) distinct from the affordances (what is possible), and could a designer remove an unnecessary label by improving the perceived affordance?
- **Source quote:** "Affordances determine what actions are possible. Signifiers communicate where the action should take place. We need both... In design, signifiers are more important than affordances, for they communicate how to use the design." (Chapter 1)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-28

### Constraint Layering Check
- **Check:** Are physical, cultural, semantic, and logical constraints all exploited so that even without instructions only one assembly / interpretation makes sense?
- **Source quote:** "These four classes of constraints—physical, cultural, semantic, and logical—seem to be universal, appearing in a wide variety of situations. Constraints are powerful clues, limiting the set of possible actions." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-28

### Mode Visibility Check
- **Check:** If the device has modes (same control, different meaning), is the current mode continuously and conspicuously visible — and could the mode be eliminated?
- **Source quote:** "Mode error is really design error. Mode errors are especially likely where the equipment does not make the mode visible, so the user is expected to remember what mode has been established, sometimes hours earlier... Designers must try to avoid modes, but if they are necessary, the equipment must make it obvious which mode is invoked." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-28

### Capture-Path Divergence Check
- **Check:** Do procedures that share opening steps diverge from the very first action, so a familiar routine cannot capture an unfamiliar one when attention lapses?
- **Source quote:** "Designers need to avoid procedures that have identical opening steps but then diverge. The more experienced the workers, the more likely they are to fall prey to capture. Whenever possible, sequences should be designed to differ from the very start." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Description-Similarity Check
- **Check:** Are easily confused controls or items made visually and physically distinct (shape-coded, separated, differently colored), so the wrong target cannot match a vague description?
- **Source quote:** "Designers need to ensure that controls and displays for different purposes are significantly different from one another. A lineup of identical-looking switches or displays is very apt to lead to description-similarity error." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-28

### Interruption Recovery Check
- **Check:** After an interruption, can the user resume by reading the current state from the system, or does the system discard the goal/plan/state the user needs to resume?
- **Source quote:** "A major source of error, especially memory-lapse errors, is interruption... Most systems make it difficult to resume after an interruption. Most discard critical information that is needed by the user to remember the numerous small decisions that had been made" (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-21, D-27

### Root-Cause Five-Whys Check
- **Check:** When an error is found, does the investigation keep asking "why?" past the first human-error finding until the systemic cause that allowed the error is exposed and redesignable?
- **Source quote:** "Root cause analysis is intended to determine the underlying cause of an incident, not the proximate cause... Basically, it means that when searching for the reason, even after you have found one, do not stop: ask why that was the case. And then ask why again. Keep asking until you have uncovered the true underlying causes." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Blame-the-Design Check
- **Check:** When many users make the same "error," is the design treated as the cause rather than the user — and is the system redesigned instead of the user retrained?
- **Source quote:** "If the system lets you make the error, it is badly designed. And if the system induces you to make the error, then it is really badly designed. When I turn on the wrong stove burner, it is not due to my lack of knowledge: it is due to poor mapping between controls and burners. Teaching me the relationship will not stop the error from recurring: redesigning the stove will." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-21, D-34

### Confirmation Dialog Quality Check
- **Check:** Does a destructive-action confirmation prominently display both the action AND the object (not just "are you sure?"), so the user can catch a slip aimed at the wrong target?
- **Source quote:** "A better check would be a prominent display of both the action to be taken and the object, perhaps with the choice of 'cancel' or 'do it.' The important point is making salient what the implications of the action are." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-2

### Inclusive-Design Edge Check
- **Check:** Does the design work for the 5th and 95th percentile (or beyond), and do adjustments for the edges benefit the middle (type size, contrast, adjustability)?
- **Source quote:** "Design for the 99th percentile of the world and 70 million people are left out... The best solution to the problem of designing for everyone is flexibility: flexibility in the size of the images on computer screens, in the sizes, heights, and angles of tables and chairs." (Chapter 6)
- **Relates to:** D-28

### Standardize-on-Psychological-Model Check
- **Check:** When standardizing a control convention (faucet rotation, scroll direction), does the standard match the user's conceptual model of the action rather than the mechanical convenience of the device?
- **Source quote:** "If you can't put the knowledge on the device (that is, knowledge in the world), then develop a cultural constraint: standardize what has to be kept in the head. And remember the lesson from faucet rotation on page 153: The standards should reflect the psychological conceptual models, not the physical mechanics." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-27, D-34

## Antipatterns

### Norman Door (Signifier-Affordance Mismatch)
- **Failure mode:** Identical hardware on push and pull sides (or no visible hardware at all) gives no cue which way the door opens; users push when they should pull and vice versa, and the design needs a sign to compensate — a confession of failure.
- **Source quote:** "When external signifiers—signs—have to be added to something as simple as a door, it indicates bad design." (Chapter 1, Figure 1.2 caption)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-28
- **Correct alternative:** Use a vertical plate where pushing is required and a pull-shaped handle where pulling is required; expose hinges/pillars so the operating side is obvious.

### False Conceptual Model (Refrigerator Controls)
- **Failure mode:** The controls suggest one control per compartment, but the mechanism is shared, so the user's mental model is wrong and adjustments produce paradoxical results; the user is blamed for "not getting it."
- **Source quote:** "Why? Because the controls suggest a false conceptual model. Two compartments, two controls, which implies that each control is responsible for the temperature of the compartment that carries its name: this conceptual model is shown in Figure 1.10A. It is wrong." (Chapter 1)
- **Relates to:** D-27
- **Correct alternative:** Match the control surface to the actual mechanism, or provide an explicit display that reveals the shared thermostat/valve relationship (Nest-style conceptual-model display).

### Mode Error (Hidden Modes)
- **Failure mode:** A single control has different meanings in different states, but the current mode is not visible, so the user executes a perfectly-formed command against the wrong mode with catastrophic results (Airbus -3.3 vs -3300).
- **Source quote:** "A mode error occurs when a device has different states in which the same controls have different meanings... Mode error is really design error. Mode errors are especially likely where the equipment does not make the mode visible" (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-28
- **Correct alternative:** Eliminate modes where possible; otherwise make the active mode continuously visible and the two modes' displays visually distinct (different digit count, color, label).

### Identical-Adjacent-Controls Antipattern
- **Failure mode:** A row of identical switches (auditorium lights, airplane flap/gear levers) guarantees description-similarity slips and capture slips; the operator hits the wrong one because nothing distinguishes them.
- **Source quote:** "One type of popular small airplane has identical-looking switches for flaps and for landing gear, right next to one another. You might be surprised to learn how many pilots, while on the ground, have decided to raise the flaps and instead raised the wheels." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-21
- **Correct alternative:** Shape-code controls (flap lever feels like a flap, gear lever like a wheel), separate them spatially, or use distinct colors/labels.

### Coin-Confusion Antipattern (Insufficient Discriminating Features)
- **Failure mode:** A new item (Susan B. Anthony dollar, UK pound coin) is introduced with size/weight too close to an existing item; users' existing imprecise mental descriptions can't distinguish them, so they confuse the two.
- **Source quote:** "The confusions probably occurred because the users of coins had already formed representations in their memories that were only sufficiently precise to distinguish among the coins that they were accustomed to using... the descriptions formed to distinguish among national currency were not precise enough to distinguish between a new coin and at least one of the old coins." (Chapter 3)
- **Relates to:** D-27, D-1
- **Correct alternative:** Introduce new items with at least one salient distinguishing dimension (size, color, edge pattern) that exceeds the discriminability threshold of existing mental descriptions.

### Beep-Only Feedback (Uninformative Alarm)
- **Failure mode:** A single cheap beeper or flashing light encodes many different states via patterns of beeps; users can't tell which device is beeping, what state it means, or what to do, so they ignore or disable the alarms.
- **Source quote:** "These simple light flashes or beeps are usually more annoying than useful. They tell us that something has happened, but convey very little information about what has happened, and then nothing about what we should do about it... Poor feedback can be worse than no feedback at all" (Chapter 1)
- **Relates to:** D-2
- **Correct alternative:** Use naturalistic sounds with rich spectra that reveal the source, and prioritize / coordinate alarms so a real emergency doesn't get tuned out.

### Excessive Alarm Antipattern
- **Failure mode:** In an emergency every device alarms simultaneously with similar tones; operators spend critical time silencing alarms instead of diagnosing, and the cacophony masks the important signal.
- **Source quote:** "In many emergencies, workers have to spend valuable time turning off all the alarms because the sounds interfere with the concentration required to solve the problem. Hospital operating rooms, emergency wards. Nuclear power control plants... All can become confusing, irritating, and life-endangering places because of excessive feedback, excessive alarms" (Chapter 1)
- **Relates to:** D-2
- **Correct alternative:** Prioritize and coordinate alarms; suppress redundant signals during an active emergency; use a coordinated alarm-management standard.

### Disappearing Information Antipattern
- **Failure mode:** Critical information (a status message, a phone number, a patient's vital sign) is displayed only briefly and then vanishes; when the user is interrupted and returns, there is nothing to read.
- **Source quote:** "Computer systems often enhance people's frustration when things go wrong by presenting critical information in a message that then disappears from the display just when the person wishes to make use of the information." (Chapter 3)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-21
- **Correct alternative:** Persist critical information until the user dismisses it, or make it trivially retrievable; design for interruption.

### Blame-the-User Antipattern
- **Failure mode:** When a system-induced error occurs, the user is blamed ("human error"), training is repeated, and the design that produced the error is unchanged — so the same error recurs in the next user.
- **Source quote:** "The idea that a person is at fault when something goes wrong is deeply entrenched in society... in my experience, human error usually is a result of poor design: it should be called system error. Humans err continually; it is an intrinsic part of our nature. System design should take this into account." (Chapter 2)
- **Relates to:** D-21
- **Correct alternative:** Treat every "human error" as a system-design signal; apply root-cause analysis and redesign so the error is harder or impossible to make.

### Stop-at-Human-Error Antipattern
- **Failure mode:** Root-cause analysis halts the moment a person is found to have acted inappropriately, never asking why the system allowed or induced that action; the deeper, redesignable cause is never addressed.
- **Source quote:** "why does the root cause analysis stop as soon as a human error is found? If a machine stops working, we don't stop the analysis when we discover a broken part. Instead, we ask: 'Why did the part break?'... We should do the same thing when we find human error" (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-21
- **Correct alternative:** Apply the Five Whys past the human-error finding, all the way to the systemic precondition.

### Featuritis / Creeping Featurism
- **Failure mode:** Each release adds features to match competitors; the product bloats until it is unusable and indistinguishable from competitors, and the original coherent activity support is buried.
- **Source quote:** "Creeping featurism is the tendency to add to the number of features of a product, often extending the number beyond all reason. There is no way that a product can remain usable and understandable by the time it has all of those special-purpose features that have been added in over time." (Chapter 7)
- **Relates to:** D-28, D-27
- **Correct alternative:** Concentrate on real strengths, ignore irrelevant competitor features, focus on the activity the user is actually performing (Moon's "Different" argument).

### Logic-of-Legacy Antipattern (Suboptimal Standard Frozen)
- **Failure mode:** A suboptimal arrangement (QWERTY, cylindrical battery, USB-A orientation) persists because switching would break compatibility, even though a known superior design exists; the legacy outlives the reason for it.
- **Source quote:** "This is called the legacy problem... Too many devices use the existing standard—that is the legacy. If the symmetrical cylindrical battery were changed, there would also have to be a major change in a huge number of products." (Chapter 4)
- **Relates to:** D-27, D-28
- **Correct alternative:** When a fresh design is possible, break the legacy; when not, design orientation-insensitive contacts (InstaLoad) or migrate via standards work, while being honest about the cost of staying.

### Out-of-Loop Automation Antipattern
- **Failure mode:** Automation handles the easy part and gives up silently on the hard part; the human, having been disengaged, is suddenly dropped back in with no awareness of state and no time to recover (Royal Majesty dead-reckoning for days).
- **Source quote:** "When automation fails, it often does so without warning... the human is 'out of the loop.' This means that the person has not been paying much attention to the operation, and it takes time for the failure to be noticed and evaluated" (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-27
- **Correct alternative:** Keep the human informed of automation state continuously; fail loudly and early; share control rather than handing off cleanly and taking it back cleanly.

### Conspiracy of Silence (Self-Blame Around Bad Design)
- **Failure mode:** Many users fail at the same device but each thinks they alone are stupid, so no one reports the problem and the design is never fixed; learned helplessness generalizes to whole categories of technology.
- **Source quote:** "Because everyone perceives the fault to be his or her own, nobody wants to admit to having trouble. This creates a conspiracy of silence, where the feelings of guilt and helplessness among people are kept hidden." (Chapter 2)
- **Relates to:** D-21
- **Correct alternative:** Build anonymous error-reporting channels (NASA ASRS model), thank reporters, and use aggregate data to redesign.

### Stigma-Driven Rejection of Assistive Design
- **Failure mode:** A device that solves a real accessibility problem is rejected because it advertises the user's infirmity; the "ugly" walker, the clinical-looking hearing aid never get used.
- **Source quote:** "Most people do not wish to advertise their infirmities... When Sam Farber wanted to develop a set of household tools that his arthritic wife could use, he worked hard to find a solution that was good for everyone. The result was a series of tools that revolutionized this field." (Chapter 6, OXO peeler story)
- **Relates to:** D-28
- **Correct alternative:** Design assistive features as universal improvements with no stigma (OXO peeler: designed for arthritis, marketed to all); use color, fashion, and pride rather than clinical drab.

### Linear/Sequential Imposition on Non-Sequential Tasks
- **Failure mode:** A printed checklist forces a strict order on tasks whose steps are actually independent; when an early step can't be done yet, users skip and forget to return, producing memory-lapse errors.
- **Source quote:** "In general, it is bad design to impose a sequential structure to task execution unless the task itself requires it. This is one of the major benefits of electronic checklists: they can keep track of skipped items and can ensure that the list will not be marked as complete until all items have been done." (Chapter 5)
- **Relates to:** D-21
- **Correct alternative:** Use electronic checklists that track completion independent of order, and only force sequencing where the task genuinely requires it.

### Rigid Format Requirement
- **Failure mode:** The system demands one exact input format and rejects all others, blaming the user for "wrong" entry even though the intent is perfectly intelligible.
- **Source quote:** "Many machines are programmed to be very fussy about the form of input they require, where the fussiness is not a requirement of the machine but due to the lack of consideration for people in the design of the software. In other words: inappropriate programming." (Chapter 2)
- **Relates to:** D-21
- **Correct alternative:** Parse any intelligible format and transform internally; only reject when the input is genuinely ambiguous.
