# B7 Williams — Patterns, Heuristics, Antipatterns

> Source: Robin Williams — *The Non-Designer's Design Book*. Quotes verbatim from extract.md; chapter numbers per source headers.

## Patterns

### Proximity grouping by intellectual relationship
- **Description:** Group items that have a logical/semantic relationship into close physical proximity, and move unrelated items apart. The closeness or distance itself encodes the relationship — readers decode it instantly without conscious effort.
- **Source quote:** "The principle of proximity states that you group related Items together, move them physically close to each other so the related items are seen as one cohesive group rather than a bunch of unrelated bits." (Chapter 1: Proximity)
- **Relates to:** D-13

### Vary spacing to signal relationship strength
- **Description:** Use unequal spacing between groups to indicate closeness/importance of the relationship. Equal spacing between everything destroys hierarchy; deliberate spacing gradients encode which groups belong together and which are subsidiary.
- **Source quote:** "If items are related to each other, group them into closer proximity. Separate items that are not directly related to each other. Vary the space between to indicate the closeness or the importance of the relationship." (Chapter 5: Review of the Four Design Principles)
- **Relates to:** D-13, D-14

### Flush-left (or flush-right) alignment for a hard edge
- **Description:** Use flush-left or flush-right alignment rather than centered alignment to produce a hard vertical edge. The invisible line connecting aligned elements gives the layout strength and a more sophisticated look than centered type, which has "soft" edges.
- **Source quote:** "If text is aligned, instead, on the left or the right, the invisible line that connects the text is much stronger because it has a hard vertical edge to follow. This gives left- and right-aligned text a cleaner and more dramatic look." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13, D-93

### One text alignment per page
- **Description:** Until experienced, use a single text alignment (all flush left, all flush right, or all centered) on a page. Multiple alignments create disconnected elements with no visual tie; one alignment produces a unified, organized look.
- **Source quote:** "Until you have more training, stick to the guideline of using one text alignment on the page: either all text is flush left, flush right, or centered." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13

### Align photo flush edge with text flush edge
- **Description:** When placing a photo or graphic next to text, align the flush (straight) side of the photo against the flush edge of the text so the two strong lines reinforce each other. Avoids trapped white space that pushes elements apart.
- **Source quote:** "Find a strong line and use it. If you have a photo or a graphic with a strong flush side, align the flush side of the text along the straight edge of the photo." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13

### Push existing repetition into a visual key
- **Description:** Find elements you already repeat consistently (headlines, bullets, rules) and amplify them into a strong, distinctive visual motif that ties the whole piece together. Repetition moves from "consistent" to a conscious unifying graphic symbol.
- **Source quote:** "Take advantage of those elements you're already using to make a project consistent and turn those elements into repetitive graphic symbols... You're taking the repetition you have already built into the project and pushing it so it is stronger and more dynamic." (Chapter 3: Repetition)
- **Relates to:** D-26

### One strong repeated element across a package
- **Description:** For multi-piece or multi-page work (business card + letterhead + envelope, or every page of a newsletter), repeat at least one strong element across all pieces so a reader instantly recognizes they belong together.
- **Source quote:** "To create a consistent business package with a business card, letterhead, and envelope, use a strong display of repetition, not only within each piece, but between all the pieces. You want the person who receives the letter to know you are the same person who gave them a business card last week." (Chapter 3: Repetition)
- **Relates to:** D-26

### Repetition with variety (unity without monotony)
- **Description:** When a repeated element is strong (e.g. a circle), repeat it in varied sizes, colors, angles, or treatments rather than verbatim. Maintains unity while avoiding monotony.
- **Source quote:** "Don't overdo it with repetition, but do try 'unity with variety.' That is, if a repetitive element is strong, such as a circle, you can repeat the circle in a variety of ways instead of repeating the exact same circle." (Chapter 3: Repetition)
- **Relates to:** D-26

### Contrast creates the focal point
- **Description:** Determine what should be the focus and make it dramatically larger/bolder/visually distinct so the eye lands there first. Everything else can be smaller — once pulled in, readers will read the small print if interested.
- **Source quote:** "Determine what you want the focus to be. Use contrast to create that focus. Enhance it with strong alignments and use of proximity... Don't be afraid to make some items small to create a contrast with the larger items, and to allow blank space!" (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14

### Contrast-over-similarity rule (really different, or identical)
- **Description:** If two elements are not exactly the same, make them really different. Sort-of-different is conflict, not contrast. Either match exactly or diverge sharply — the middle is the failure mode.
- **Source quote:** "If the two elements are sort of different, but not really, then you don't have contrast, you have conflict. That's the key—the principle of contrast states that If two Items are not exactly the same, then make them different. Really different." (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14

### Combine contrast with repetition for unifying identity
- **Description:** Use contrasting elements as repeated elements across a publication (headline typeface, dark bands, bullet style, column direction). Contrast attracts the eye; repetition makes the contrast serve unity.
- **Source quote:** "Combine contrast with repetition, as in the page numbers or headlines or bullets or rules or spatial arrangements, to make a strong, unifying identity throughout an entire publication." (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14, D-26

### Direction contrast (horizontal vs vertical)
- **Description:** Contrast a long horizontal element (title bar) against tall, narrow vertical columns to create directional tension on the page. The narrow columns can themselves become a repetitive element.
- **Source quote:** "Besides the contrast in the typefaces in this postcard, there is also a contrast between the long, horizontal title and the tall, narrow, vertical columns. The narrow columns are a repetitive element, as well as an example of contrast." (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14

## Heuristics

### Count the visual stops (squint test)
- **Check:** Squint at the page and count how many times your eye stops — i.e. how many separate visual elements exist. If there are more than three to five, group related items into closer proximity until they become one visual unit.
- **Source quote:** "Squint your eyes slightly and count the number of visual elements on the page by counting the number of times your eye stops. If there are more than three to five items on the page (of course it depends on the piece), see which of the separate elements can be grouped together into closer proximity to become one visual unit." (Chapter 1: Proximity)
- **Relates to:** D-13, D-14

### Does each item have a visual connection?
- **Check:** For every element on the page, ask: is it visually aligned with some other element? Nothing should be placed arbitrarily — there should be an invisible line connecting it to another item, even across physical distance.
- **Source quote:** "Nothing should be placed on the page arbitrarily. Every Item should have a visual connection with something else on the page... When items are aligned on the page, it creates a stronger cohesive unit. Even when aligned elements are physically separated from each other, there is an invisible line that connects them, both in your eye and in your mind." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13

### Can you draw the alignment lines?
- **Check:** On a well-designed piece you should be able to draw visible lines along the aligned objects. If alignments are weak, missing, or multiple-and-unconnected, the page will feel messy even if the cause is hard to articulate.
- **Source quote:** "In any well-designed piece, you will be able to draw lines to the aligned objects, even if the overall presentation of material is a wild collection of odd things and has lots of energy." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13

### Is the contrast strong or wimpy?
- **Check:** For every pair of elements that are not the same, ask: is the difference strong enough that no one could mistake it for an error? Contrast 12pt with 14pt? Half-point rule with one-point rule? Dark brown with black? All fail — push the difference until it is unmistakable.
- **Source quote:** "But don't be a wimp. You cannot contrast 12-point type with 14-point type. You cannot contrast a half-point rule with a one-point rule. You cannot contrast dark brown with black. Get serious." (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14

### Is centered alignment a conscious choice or a default?
- **Check:** Centered alignment is the most common beginner default and reads as formal, sedate, often dull. Ask: did I center this because the content calls for formality, or because it felt safe? If the latter, try a strong flush left or right.
- **Source quote:** "Do you tend to automatically center everything? A centered alignment is the most common alignment that beginners use-it's very safe, it feels comfortable. A centered alignment creates a more formal look, a more sedate look, a more ordinary and oftentimes downright dull look." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-93

### Is every page obviously part of the same publication?
- **Check:** In a multi-page document, open two non-adjacent pages side by side. It should be instantly obvious they belong to the same piece. If page 7 has no repetitive element carried from page 6, the cohesive look is broken.
- **Source quote:** "When readers open the document, it should be perfectly and instantly obvious that page 7 and page 12 are really part of the same publication." (Chapter 3: Repetition)
- **Relates to:** D-26

### Is trapped white space pushing elements apart?
- **Check:** Look for white space "trapped" between elements in awkward shapes — between a text block and a photo, or between a headline and its paragraph. Trapped space pushes elements apart visually; re-align to free the space off an edge.
- **Source quote:** "There is a nice strong line along the left edge of the type. There is a nice strong line along the left edge of the 'photograph.' Between the text and the photo, though, there is 'trapped' white space, and the white space is an awkward shape. When white space is trapped, it pushes the two elements apart." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13

### Is there a clear focal point?
- **Check:** Glance at the page for one second. Where did your eye land? If nothing drew the eye — everything similar in size, weight, and importance — there is no focal point. Decide what should be the focus and use contrast to create it.
- **Source quote:** "One of the problems is that everything is basically the same size and weight and importance; 'Builders Exchange Member' is as important, visually, as 'Remodel and Repair Specialists.' But should it be? Determine what you want the focus to be. Use contrast to create that focus." (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14

### Does the headline belong to the text above or below?
- **Check:** A headline should be unambiguously closer to the paragraph it heads than to the preceding paragraph. If distances are equal above and below, the reader cannot tell which text the headline belongs to — group it with its related text.
- **Source quote:** "The person who designed this mini-poster typed two Returns after each headline and paragraph. Thus the headlines are each the same distance from the body copy above and below, so they appear to be separate, unconnected items. You can't tell if the headline belongs to the text above it or below it because the distances are the same." (Chapter 1: Proximity)
- **Relates to:** D-13

## Antipatterns

### Equal white space between everything
- **Failure mode:** Leaving equal amounts of white space between all elements destroys grouping signals — every item appears equidistant from every other, so no relationship can be read. The page reads as unorganized even when "neat."
- **Source quote:** "Avoid leaving equal amounts of white space between elements unless each group is part of a subset." (Chapter 1: Proximity)
- **Relates to:** D-13
- **Correct alternative:** Vary the spacing deliberately — tight within a group, generous between groups — to encode which items belong together.

### Items scattered into the corners
- **Failure mode:** Distributing elements into corners and the middle to "fill the space" produces a scattered page with no organization and no clear reading order. The reader's eye wanders and may miss content.
- **Source quote:** "Very often in beginners' designs, the words and phrases and graphics are strung out all over the place, filling corners and taking up lots of room so there won't be any empty space. There seems to be a fear of empty space. When pieces of a design are scattered all over, the page appears unorganized and the information may not be instantly accessible to the reader." (Chapter 1: Proximity)
- **Relates to:** D-13, D-94
- **Correct alternative:** Group related items into proximity and let empty space collect at the margins as organized white space.

### Centered headlines over flush-left body
- **Failure mode:** Centering a headline above flush-left body copy (especially with indented paragraphs) breaks alignment — the headline has no common edge with the text and appears to "just hang around." The page reads as a collection of misaligned spots.
- **Source quote:** "Never center headlines over flush left body copy or text that has an indent! If the text does not have a clear left and right edge, you can't tell the headline is actually centered. It looks like it's just hanging around." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-93, D-13
- **Correct alternative:** Set heads and subheads flush left to match flush-left body; maintain a single strong alignment line.

### Multiple alignments on one page with no shared edge
- **Failure mode:** Mixing flush-left, flush-right, and centered elements on a single small piece (e.g. a business card) with no common edge creates three disconnected groups. The elements look thrown on; there is no invisible line tying them together.
- **Source quote:** "Part of its problem is that nothing is aligned with anything else. In this little space, there are elements with three different alignments: flush left, flush right, and centered. The two groups of text in the upper corners are not lined up along the same baseline, nor are they aligned at the left or right edges with the two groups at the bottom of the card." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13
- **Correct alternative:** Pick one alignment and move everything to it; or, if mixing, ensure a shared edge connects the two alignment groups.

### Sort-of-different elements (conflict, not contrast)
- **Failure mode:** Two elements that are slightly different (12pt vs 14pt, half-point vs one-point rule, dark brown vs black) read as a mistake rather than as contrast. The eye senses something is off but cannot decide if it was intentional.
- **Source quote:** "If the two elements are sort of different, but not really, then you don't have contrast, you have conflict... 'Almost' only counts in horseshoes and hand grenades." (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14
- **Correct alternative:** Make the difference dramatic and unmistakable, or make the elements identical.

### Everything the same size, weight, and importance
- **Failure mode:** A page where every element has similar visual weight has no focal point — nothing draws the eye, so nothing gets read first. Common in phone-book ads and resumes where job titles blend into body text.
- **Source quote:** "One of the problems is that everything is basically the same size and weight and importance; 'Builders Exchange Member' is as important, visually, as 'Remodel and Repair Specialists.' But should it be?" (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14
- **Correct alternative:** Decide what should be the focus and make it dramatically larger/bolder; let supporting items recede.

### Wimpy rule contrast (half-point vs one-point)
- **Failure mode:** Using a hairline rule in one place and a slightly thicker rule elsewhere on the same page creates ambiguity — are the two thicknesses intentional or a printing error? Weak contrast reads as a mistake.
- **Source quote:** "If you use a hairline rule between columns, use a strong 2- or 4-point rule when you need another-don't use a half-point rule and a one-point rule on the same page." (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-14
- **Correct alternative:** Make rule-weight differences large and obvious, or use the same weight throughout.

### Repetition overdone (red everything)
- **Failure mode:** Repeating a strong element so many times and in so many places that it overwhelms the piece and confuses the focus. A red hat, earrings, lipstick, handbag, shoes, and coat together stop being a unifying accent and become the entire message.
- **Source quote:** "If the woman were to wear the black evening dress with a red hat, red earrings, red lipstick, a red handbag, red shoes and a red coat, the repetition would not be a stunning and unifying contrast-it would be overwhelming and the focus would be confused." (Chapter 3: Repetition)
- **Relates to:** D-26
- **Correct alternative:** Repeat the element enough to unify, not enough to dominate; use "unity with variety" — vary size, angle, or treatment.

### Overpowering border competing with the message
- **Failure mode:** A heavy border or decorative frame draws more attention than the content it encloses. The decoration becomes the focal point instead of the information.
- **Source quote:** "Remove conflicting elements: The border is not a focal point-why make it so overpowering? The stars call too much attention to themselves-focus the attention on the purpose of the ad." (Chapter 4: Contrast)
- **Relates to:** D-94
- **Correct alternative:** Strip the border or lighten it; let the actual focal point carry the visual weight.

### Timid alignment break
- **Failure mode:** Breaking an alignment half-heartedly so it looks accidental rather than intentional. A conscious break can be powerful; a timid break reads as an error.
- **Source quote:** "If your alignments are strong, you can break through them consciously and it will look intentional. The trick is you cannot be timid about breaking the alignment-either do it all the way or don't do it. Don't be a wimp." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13
- **Correct alternative:** Either commit to a full, obvious break or keep the alignment intact.

### Subtle misalignments adding up
- **Failure mode:** Each small misalignment (a caption centered under a flush-left photo, a headline not aligned to the text column, a graphic hanging slightly over the edge) is individually minor, but together they produce a visually messy page — the "slightly messy kitchen" effect.
- **Source quote:** "All those minor misalignments add up to create a visually messy page. Find a strong line and stick to it. Even though it may be subtle and your boss couldn't say what made the difference between this example and the one before it, the more sophisticated look comes through clearly." (Chapter 2: Alignment)
- **Relates to:** D-13
- **Correct alternative:** Audit every element for alignment with a shared edge; "find a strong line and stick to it."
