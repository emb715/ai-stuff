# Patterns — UI/UX Solutions Mapped to Laws

Consolidated from B1–B9. Each pattern = one named, reusable UI/UX solution. Grouped by cluster.

---

## Perception

### Proximity Grouping
- **Description:** Group related items close together; separate unrelated items with more space. Try spacing before lines or boxes.
- **Source:** "If you want items to be seen as belonging together, then put them in proximity. Before you use lines or boxes to separate items or group them together, first try experimenting with the amount of space between them." — B5, Thing 9
- **Relates to:** D-5, D-13, D-29

### Single-Feature Pop-Out
- **Description:** Make the most important element differ in exactly one visual feature (color, shape, or orientation); combining many features weakens pop-out.
- **Source:** "In an image or on a page, whatever item is a different color, shape, or orientation than the others is the item that will grab attention first." — B5, Thing 5
- **Relates to:** D-30

### Redundant Color Coding
- **Description:** Where color carries meaning, add a redundant cue (line thickness, pattern, icon, box) so the encoding survives color blindness.
- **Source:** "Wherever you use color to give specific meaning, you need a redundant coding scheme—for example, color and line thickness—so that people who are color-blind will be able to decipher the code." — B5, Thing 11
- **Relates to:** D-30, D-62

### Scan-Anchor Placement
- **Description:** Place the most important task information ~30% in from the top and ~30% in from the left; reserve edges for branding.
- **Source:** "Put the most important information about 30 percent of the page or screen from the top and 30 percent from the left margin." — B5, Thing 6
- **Relates to:** D-33

### Affordance Cues via Shading
- **Description:** Use shadows, shading, and depth to signal that an element can be pressed/activated; never provide cues that conflict with behavior.
- **Source:** "Use shading to show when an object is chosen or active. Avoid providing incorrect affordance cues." — B5, Thing 7
- **Relates to:** D-1, D-27, D-34

### Visual Hierarchy of Prominence/Grouping/Nesting
- **Description:** Make visual appearance reflect relationships: more important = more prominent; related grouped together; parent/child via nesting.
- **Source:** "Pages with a clear visual hierarchy have three traits: The more important something is, the more prominent it is... Things that are related logically are related visually... Things are 'nested' visually." — B2, Chapter 3
- **Relates to:** D-14, D-83

### Figure-Ground for Focus
- **Description:** Differentiate figure from ground to focus attention and aid recall; give key elements definite shape in lower regions.
- **Source:** "Clearly differentiate between figure and ground in order to focus attention and minimize perceptual confusion." — B6, Figure-Ground
- **Relates to:** D-14, D-79

### Contrast Creates the Focal Point
- **Description:** Decide what should be the focus and make it dramatically larger/bolder/distinct; let supporting items recede.
- **Source:** "Determine what you want the focus to be. Use contrast to create that focus... Don't be afraid to make some items small to create a contrast with the larger items." — B7, Chapter 4
- **Relates to:** D-14

## Cognition

### Chunk to Four
- **Description:** When you cannot limit items to 3–4, group into 3–4 chunks with no more than 4 items each; phone-number formatting is canonical.
- **Source:** "If you can't limit the number of links, topics, or choices to three or four, chunk or group information into three or four groups with no more than four items in each chunk." — B5, Thing 20
- **Relates to:** D-8, D-83

### Recognition Over Recall
- **Description:** Offer dropdown lists, auto-fill, thumbnails, and visible choices instead of forcing users to recall from memory.
- **Source:** "Eliminate memory load whenever possible. Make use of user interface features such as auto-fill and dropdown lists to reduce the need for people to recall items from memory." — B5, Thing 22
- **Relates to:** D-9, D-32, D-44

### Progressive Disclosure
- **Description:** Show people only what they need at the moment and let them click for more; count useful clicks, not total clicks.
- **Source:** "Use progressive disclosure. Show people what they need when they need it. If you have to make a trade-off on clicks versus thinking, use more clicks and less thinking." — B5, Thing 27
- **Relates to:** D-29

### Load-Tier Trade-Offs
- **Description:** Rank design demands as cognitive > visual > motor; trade a cognitive load for a visual or motor load whenever possible.
- **Source:** "Look for trade-offs (for example, where you can reduce a cognitive load by increasing a visual or motor load)." — B5, Thing 28
- **Relates to:** D-29, D-34

### Title-First Comprehension
- **Description:** Provide a meaningful title or headline before body content; the title frames comprehension.
- **Source:** "Provide a meaningful title or headline. It's one of the most important things you can do." — B5, Thing 14
- **Relates to:** D-56

### Concrete Terms and Icons
- **Description:** Use concrete words and concrete icons; they are easier to encode and remember than abstractions.
- **Source:** "Use concrete terms and icons. They will be easier to remember than abstract ideas or images." — B5, Thing 23
- **Relates to:** D-42

### Three-or-Four Choice Curation
- **Description:** Limit choices to 3–4, or use progressive choice (small set then subset); resist offering many choices at once.
- **Source:** "Resist the impulse to provide your customers with a large number of choices. If possible, limit the number of choices to three or four. If you have to offer more options, try to do so in a progressive way." — B5, Thing 92
- **Relates to:** D-8, D-10

## Composition

### Flush-Left/Flush-Right Alignment
- **Description:** Use flush-left or flush-right rather than centered alignment to produce a hard vertical edge; stronger, cleaner, more sophisticated.
- **Source:** "If text is aligned on the left or the right, the invisible line that connects the text is much stronger because it has a hard vertical edge to follow." — B7, Chapter 2
- **Relates to:** D-13, D-93

### One Alignment Per Page
- **Description:** Use a single text alignment on a page; multiple disconnected alignments destroy unity.
- **Source:** "Stick to the guideline of using one text alignment on the page: either all text is flush left, flush right, or centered." — B7, Chapter 2
- **Relates to:** D-13

### Repetition as Visual Key
- **Description:** Find elements already repeated consistently and amplify them into a strong, distinctive motif that ties the whole piece together.
- **Source:** "Take advantage of those elements you're already using to make a project consistent and turn those elements into repetitive graphic symbols." — B7, Chapter 3
- **Relates to:** D-26

### Repetition with Variety
- **Description:** When a repeated element is strong, repeat it in varied sizes/colors/angles rather than verbatim to maintain unity without monotony.
- **Source:** "Don't overdo it with repetition, but do try 'unity with variety.' If a repetitive element is strong, such as a circle, you can repeat the circle in a variety of ways." — B7, Chapter 3
- **Relates to:** D-26

### Signal-to-Noise Maximization
- **Description:** Maximize signal by keeping designs simple and emphasizing key info via redundant coding/highlighting; minimize noise by removing unnecessary elements.
- **Source:** "Seek to maximize the signal-to-noise ratio in design. Increase signal by keeping designs simple... Minimize noise by removing unnecessary elements." — B6, Signal-to-Noise Ratio
- **Relates to:** D-30, D-74

### Clearly Defined Page Areas
- **Description:** Divide pages into well-defined regions (nav, content, promos) so users can quickly decide which to focus on and which to ignore.
- **Source:** "Dividing the page into clearly defined areas is important because it allows users to decide quickly which areas of the page to focus on and which areas they can safely ignore." — B2, Chapter 3
- **Relates to:** D-14

### Scan-Friendly Text Formatting
- **Description:** Format for scanning: plenty of headings, short paragraphs, bulleted lists, bold key terms where they first appear.
- **Source:** "Use plenty of headings... Keep paragraphs short... Use bulleted lists... Highlight key terms." — B2, Chapter 3
- **Relates to:** D-11

## Interaction

### Forcing Function (Sequence Interlock)
- **Description:** Block the next stage of an action until a prior condition is satisfied so an omitted prerequisite becomes physically impossible.
- **Source:** "An interlock forces operations to take place in proper sequence. Microwave ovens use interlocks as forcing functions to prevent people from opening the door without first turning off the electric power." — B1, Chapter 4
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Undo as Primary Error Recovery
- **Description:** Provide multi-level undo so every reversible action can be taken back; treat it as the default recovery path.
- **Source:** "Perhaps the most powerful tool to minimize the impact of errors is the Undo command... The best systems have multiple levels of undoing." — B1, Chapter 5
- **Relates to:** D-2, D-21

### Sensibility Checks Before Destructive Actions
- **Description:** Have the system flag values wildly out of expected range (1000× drug dose, wrong-currency transfer) before executing.
- **Source:** "Even simple checks for reasonableness would have stopped all of these errors... Electronic systems can check to make sure that the requested operation is sensible." — B1, Chapter 5
- **Relates to:** D-21

### Two-Step Confirmation (Arm-Fire)
- **Description:** For critical/irreversible actions, use a preliminary arming step before the actual command to prevent accidental activation.
- **Source:** "The two-step operation involves a preliminary step that must occur prior to the actual command—first you arm the component, and then you fire (execute) it." — B6, Confirmation
- **Relates to:** D-35, D-37

### Fitts's-Law-Sized Targets
- **Description:** Make frequently-used or critical controls large and near; place dangerous or infrequent controls farther away and smaller.
- **Source:** "Make sure that controls are near or large, particularly when rapid movements are required and accuracy is important." — B6, Fitts' Law
- **Relates to:** D-10, D-34, D-36

### Permissive Input Parsing
- **Description:** Accept any intelligible format for dates, times, phone numbers and transform internally; never demand one exact machine format.
- **Source:** "Why not figure out the variety of ways a person might fill out a form and accommodate all of them? It even accepts phrases such as 'a week from Thursday'." — B1, Chapter 2
- **Relates to:** D-21

### See-and-Choose Over Recall-and-Type
- **Description:** Show users their options and let them choose rather than forcing them to recall and type.
- **Source:** "See and choose is easier than recall and type. Show users their options and let them choose among them, rather than force users to recall their options." — B9, Chapter 9
- **Relates to:** D-9

### Always-Visible Progress Indicator
- **Description:** Provide progress indicators so users know how long a task will take; consistent durations let users calibrate expectations.
- **Source:** "Always provide progress indicators so people know how long something will take." — B5, Thing 36
- **Relates to:** D-31, D-53

### Progressive / Important-First Rendering
- **Description:** Display important information first, then details, so users can begin planning while the rest loads; users prefer progressive results to progress indicators.
- **Source:** "Interactive systems can appear to be operating fast by displaying important information first... Research indicates that users prefer progressive results to progress indicators." — B9, Chapter 12
- **Relates to:** D-20

## Decision

### Convention Following
- **Description:** Use established conventions (logo top-left, search icon, cart metaphor) instead of reinventing; innovate only when you have a demonstrably better idea.
- **Source:** "My recommendation: Innovate when you know you have a better idea, but take advantage of conventions when you don't." — B2, Chapter 3
- **Relates to:** D-22

### Strong Information Scent at Choice Points
- **Description:** At each decision point, label options with words that literally match users' goals; avoid generic labels like OK/Cancel.
- **Source:** "Interactive systems should be designed so that the scent is strong and really does lead users to their goals." — B9, Chapter 8
- **Relates to:** D-9

### Preserve Alternative Paths
- **Description:** Provide more than one way to accomplish a task even if some are less efficient; people equate choice with control.
- **Source:** "You may want to offer more than one way, even if the alternative methods are less efficient, just so people will have a choice. Once you've given people choices, they'll be unhappy if you take those choices away." — B5, Thing 93
- **Relates to:** D-48

### Easy Shortcuts and Safe Defaults
- **Description:** Provide shortcuts that are easy to learn and find; provide defaults when you know what most people want and accidental default cost is low.
- **Source:** "Provide shortcuts as long as they are easy to learn, find, and use... Provide defaults if you know what most people will want to do most of the time and if the result of choosing a default by mistake does not cause costly errors." — B5, Thing 58
- **Relates to:** D-55

### Feature Fake / Button-to-Nowhere
- **Description:** Ship the appearance of a feature before building it to measure demand; the UI element is an experiment instrument.
- **Source:** "It is cheaper and faster to learn about the value of the idea to create the appearance of the feature where none actually exists. HTML buttons, calls to action, and other prompts and links provide the illusion that a feature exists." — B4, Ch 5
- **Relates to:** D-24

### Standardization as Last Resort
- **Description:** When natural mapping and in-world knowledge can't solve a consistent interaction problem, standardize so people learn once and transfer.
- **Source:** "If all else fails, standardize... Standardization is the fundamental principle of desperation: when no other solution appears possible, simply design everything the same way, so people only have to learn once." — B1, Chapter 4
- **Relates to:** D-27, D-28

## Cross-Cutting

### Universal / Inclusive Design
- **Description:** Design for the extremes (arthritis, low vision) and the result benefits everyone; "design for the edges, help the middle."
- **Source:** "Designing for people with special needs is often called inclusive or universal design. Those names are fitting, for it is often the case that everyone benefits." — B1, Chapter 6
- **Relates to:** D-28

### Forgiveness Hierarchy
- **Description:** Achieve forgiveness preferentially through good affordances, reversibility (undo), and safety nets; these reduce the need for confirmations, warnings, and help.
- **Source:** "The preferred methods of achieving forgiveness in a design are affordances, reversibility of actions, and safety nets. Designs that effectively use these strategies require minimal confirmations, warnings, and help." — B6, Forgiveness
- **Relates to:** D-35, D-37
